Marketing Filters

A clearer way to narrow the marketing catalog. Agents and the people who order for them were hunting through filters whose labels didn’t match the words they already used — postcards, flyers, brochures — and whose combinations didn’t behave the way they expected.

01 — Overview

The catalog, and the job of the filters

IgniteRE Marketing is where eXp Realty agents order marketing materials. In the product it is labeled Spotlight. The catalog is a grid of postcards, flyers, door hangers, brochures, and the rest of an agent’s print and social kit, with search, sort, and a filter column on the right.

The project set out to streamline that filtering: an interface with clear categorization and smart search, so agents spend less time searching and feel more confident that they have the right asset.

What this case study covers

  • Pendo usage of the marketing product filters: which categories and which individual filters were used most and least.
  • A UserZoom survey with an open card sort, plus questions on navigation and ease.
  • The rule people described for combining filters: OR inside a category, AND across categories.
  • A regrouped taxonomy that names formats the way the homepage already does.

What the document does not include

  • A timeline, a team roster, or a personal role breakdown.
  • Wireframe frames. Figma and Miro are named; the frames themselves are not in the deck.
  • A usability test, task times, or a before-and-after outcome.
  • The wording of all nine survey questions, or readable text from the interview canvases.

02 — Problem

The words on the homepage and the words in the filters were not the same words

On the marketing home page, people click categories such as Postcards and Flyers. Inside the product filters, those same materials were labeled by dimension: “small” for postcards and “8.5 × 11” for flyers. The deck calls this out directly. Postcards and Flyers were the two most-used homepage categories, and they did not appear in the filter list the way the other categories did.

All Products was the most-clicked feature inside Marketing. The deck reads that as a sign that filtering was not doing its job: people were stepping past the filters and into the full catalog.

“Postcards and Flyers are the 2 most clicked on categories on the Home Page, but they don’t appear anywhere in the product filters like the other categories do (instead it says ‘small’ for postcards and ‘8.5 × 11’ for flyers).”

03 — Goals and success criteria

Less searching. More confidence in the asset.

The project states one aim: streamline filtering with an intuitive interface, clear categorization, and smart search. That should reduce the time agents spend searching and increase their confidence that they have selected the right marketing asset.

The document does not define a numeric target and does not report whether the aim was met. The criteria below are the goal, restated as things the research already measured, so a later check would have a baseline. They are not results.

How success could be read, using baselines already in the deck

  • Ease. The survey’s average customer ease score for finding materials with the filters was 5.73 on a scale of 1 (difficult) to 7 (easy).
  • A real path through filters. All Products was the most-clicked feature in Marketing, at 12,530 clicks. A working filter set should be a credible alternative to opening everything.
  • Matching language. Homepage categories and filter labels should use the same names for the same materials.
  • Predictable combinations. People described OR within a category and AND between categories. Success is the UI behaving that way, and saying so.

05 — Research questions

Two questions, asked of the people who order

The deck states the questions the project wanted to answer:

  1. How do users want to navigate through various filters to narrow down their options for marketing materials?
  2. What are people’s mental models when organizing marketing materials into categories — and how do they want to label and represent these categories?

Who the study set out to include

  • Real estate agents — core users of IgniteRE Marketing.
  • Sales reps — likely to use IgniteRE Marketing to order marketing materials for their agents.

Who actually responded

  • 11 respondents: 6 sales reps, 3 sales managers, 1 product analyst, 1 real estate agent.
  • Locations printed on the slide: AZ (3), TX (2), FL (2), OH, HI, NM, NV. There is an empty spot in the punctuation between HI and NM, with no state name in it.

One agent, in a study aimed at agents and the reps who order for them. That composition is reported as printed. It is a limit on how far the card sort can speak for agents.

06 — Method

A UserZoom survey, with an open card sort

11Respondents. 6 sales reps, 3 sales managers, 1 product analyst, 1 real estate agent.
9Survey questions, on frequency of usage, navigation preferences, pain points, and a customer ease score. The nine wordings are not listed.
30Cards. Pieces of marketing material from Marketing / faPrint, across sizes, themes, and holidays. One example in the deck is “Welcome Home Tag.”

Card sorting was used to see how people conceptualize and group the materials. Each card was a label for a piece of marketing material. Participants sorted the cards into groups that matched their own mental model, and they wrote their own labels for those groups. It was an open sort, not a sort into categories the team had already named.

Survey questions sat alongside the sort to gauge navigation preferences, including what people expect to happen when they use more than one filter. The deck does not print those questions one by one.

Where the notes went

The research-analysis board is a Miro canvas. Its own instructions say to fill an interview canvas with yellow notes for key information and black notes for quotes, then tag a note as a pain (pink), a delight (blue), or an opportunity (green). The export is a grid of those canvases. The note text is too small to read, so this case study does not quote participants and does not invent tags.

Process. The deck has a Process heading and no diagram under it. The sequence here is the order of the document itself, not a figure from the file: Pendo usage, then the two research questions, then the UserZoom survey and open card sort, then the ease and device results, then the filter rule, then the annotated taxonomy.

07 — Synthesis and insights

Size first. Desktop. Ease at 5.73, with a long tail.

The card sort asked people to make their own groups. Most of them grouped by size before anything else. The chart title in the deck says “cart sort,” which is the card sort.

Finding materials was middling, not hard for everyone and not easy for everyone

The average customer ease score for using the filters in IRE Marketing or faPrint was 5.73. The scale runs from 1, difficult, to 7, easy. The bars sit on the gridlines: five responses at 7, three at 6, one at 5, one at 4, none at 3 or 2, and one at 1. Those eleven ratings average to 5.73. Most of the group found it easy. One person rated it as difficult as the scale allows. The deck does not say what that person struggled with.

Ordering happens on a desktop

Respondents said they mostly use a desktop or laptop to order. The survey chart shows 11 for desktop or laptop, 1 for mobile phone, and 0 for tablet. The question is worded “which device(s),” so a person could select more than one. Pendo, for January 24, 2022 through January 24, 2023, agrees in scale: 25,731 desktop visits, 1,802 mobile, and 90 tablet.

What the findings support

  • People reach for format first. Six of eleven sorts were size-first, and Size was the second-most-used filter category, behind Theme.
  • The labels they are offered for format do not match the homepage, where Postcard alone was clicked 12,227 times.
  • Photo-count filters occupy a column and almost no behavior. Several of them are the least-used controls in the product.
  • Tag is given homepage space and is not used, on the homepage or in the filters.
  • The work of ordering is a desktop task in both the survey and the year of Pendo data. A wide filter column is a plausible layout for that context. It is not evidence about a phone layout.

08 — Define

Two people, one catalog, a shared labeling problem

These portraits use only the roles and behaviors in the document. They are not interview transcripts. The study did not publish quotes.

Real estate agent

Orders their own materials

Named as the core user of IgniteRE Marketing. One of the eleven respondents was an agent, so the sort is thin evidence about agents specifically.

The job the project names for them: spend less time searching the catalog, and feel more sure of the asset they pick. The homepage already speaks their language — Postcard, Flyer, Brochure. The filters, in the research export, answered with dimensions.

Sales rep

Orders materials for their agents

Named as someone likely to use IgniteRE Marketing on an agent’s behalf. Six respondents were sales reps and three were sales managers, so this is the group the survey mostly heard from.

They are narrowing a catalog for someone else’s listing, often from a desktop. A wrong combination of filters costs a second pass through All Products. The back-to-home control was the third-most-clicked item in Marketing, at 7,405 clicks.

Needs the evidence will support

  • Find a format first, because that is how most of this group organized the cards, and how the highest homepage categories are structured.
  • See Postcards and Flyers called Postcards and Flyers inside the filters, not only on the homepage.
  • Combine two sizes without the catalog treating that as “must be both.”
  • Add a theme or a season and have that narrow the set, rather than replace it.
  • Skip a long list of photo counts that the usage data says almost no one opens.

How might we

Written from the findings as design framing. The document does not include a workshop list of these statements.

  • How might we label a filter with the same noun the homepage already uses for that material?
  • How might we let someone pick jumbo or small, and then keep only the themes they also chose?
  • How might we give Size and Theme the room the click data says they earn, and stop giving Tag and photo counts the same visual weight?
  • How might we make All Products a choice, rather than the path people take when the filters are hard to trust?

09 — Information architecture

OR inside a category. AND across categories.

The solution slide states the rule in one sentence. People described wanting OR logic for filters within the same category, and AND logic for filters between categories.

Jumbo and Small, both inside Size, means materials that are either size. Adding Property Marketing, in Theme, keeps only the materials that are one of those sizes and also that theme. Holiday, later annotated as Season, works the same way against the other groups.

10 — Ideate

The flow the research describes

Not a wireframe from the file. Figma and Miro are the tools named in the document. No sketch or wireframe frame is included. The flow below is the path implied by the homepage categories, the All Products escape hatch, and the filter rule.

  1. 1Land in MarketingChoose a homepage category — Postcard and Flyer are the heavily used ones — or open All Products.
  2. 2Read the setThe catalog in the deck shows a result total, here 252, and a count on each filter option.
  3. 3NarrowInside one category, selections add (OR). Across categories, they restrict (AND).
  4. 4RecoverClear resets the column. Back to home was a heavily used control, at 7,405 clicks, so leaving the grid has to stay obvious.

The design moves that are actually drawn are small and specific. Name the format. Group sports. Prefer Season as the label over Holiday. Collapse the photo counts that usage says people do not open. Leave EDDM as one checkbox, since it was used — 954 clicks — and does not need a new group.

    Interaction notes that follow from the rule

    Design rationale. The document does not draw filter chips, an empty state, or an accessibility specification. These are the behaviors the rule and the screens imply.

    • Say the combination in words. After a selection, a line such as “Jumbo or Small, and Property Marketing” makes OR and AND visible. Color alone, like the red arrows on the slide, will not do it.
    • Chips are a reading of the current filters, not a second menu. Each chip removes one selection. Clear removes the set. The screenshot already has Clear; it does not show chips.
    • Empty results need a cause. If AND across categories returns nothing, the message should say that every selected category has to match, and offer removal of the last category. No empty state is drawn in the deck.
    • Counts update with the set. The parenthetical counts on the screenshot are only trustworthy if they describe the current result, not the unfiltered catalog. The document does not say which of those they are.

    Accessibility, as a requirement of this UI rather than a finding

    • The filter column is a set of groups. Each group needs a name (Size, Theme, Season), and each checkbox needs a name that includes the format, not only a dimension.
    • See more and See less are buttons with an expanded or collapsed state, not a text link that moves focus unpredictably.
    • When the result total changes, that change should be announced. The screenshot shows the number visually, next to All Products.
    • The logic cannot depend on the red OR and AND arrows. Those are annotations. The interface needs the same information in text.
    • Desktop is the documented context — 25,731 desktop visits against 1,802 mobile and 90 tablet in the dated Pendo chart — so the wide column is justified. Those mobile visits still need a filter control that is not a desktop sidebar copied onto a phone. No mobile layout is in the document.

    12 — Validate

    What was checked, and what was not

    The evaluative work in the document is the research itself: Pendo usage, the ease score, the device question, and the card sort. There is no usability test, no task-time study, and no report of the filters in front of agents after the taxonomy changed.

    A follow-up that stayed honest to the baselines already collected would repeat four checks. None of these have a recorded “after.”

    Ease, againThe same question: ease of finding materials in IRE Marketing or faPrint, 1 to 7. The current average is 5.73, with one rating at 1.
    All Products as a fallbackIt led Marketing at 12,530 clicks. If the filters are trusted, that control should not have to outrank Postcard.
    Language matchCan a person move from the homepage category Postcard to a filter that is also called a postcard, without translating “small” or “4.25 × 5.5”?
    The rule, said backGiven Jumbo and Small plus one theme, does the person expect either size, restricted by the theme? That is the behavior the solution slide says people described. It was not re-tested in the deck.

    13 — Outcome

    A rule and a vocabulary. Not a measured lift.

    The document ends on the filter rule and the annotated taxonomy. That is the outcome it records. It does not record a change in time-on-task, a change in the ease score, a change in All Products clicks, or a launch date.

    What it does establish is a design decision with the evidence attached:

    • Combine filters with OR inside a category and AND between categories, because that is the navigation model people described.
    • Lead with format, because six of eleven card sorts grouped by size first and the homepage is already organized that way. Postcard, 12,227. Flyer, 4,314.
    • Use those homepage names inside the filters. The annotation maps Small and Jumbo to Postcards, 8.5 × 11 to Flyers, the brochure sizes to Brochures, and the booklet sizes to Booklets.
    • Group Football, Baseball, and Hockey as Sports. Put Season in place of the Holiday heading. Collapse photo counts behind See more. Leave EDDM alone.
    • Treat Tag as a question, not as a default. It has homepage placement and 15 homepage clicks, and 21 clicks as a size filter.

    The catalog screenshot shows that product-type naming, option counts, Clear, search, a result total, and See less can live in the existing Marketing layout. The document does not connect that screenshot to a release metric.

    14 — Learnings and next steps

    Labels were doing as much work as the logic

    The click data and the card sort point at the same decision. People think in formats, and they also think in themes: Theme was the most-clicked filter category, at 12,260, while size-first was the most common card-sort strategy. The interface has to support both without making them look like one list.

    The painful part was not the absence of filters. It was a homepage and a filter column that described the same object two ways, plus a combination rule nobody could see. All Products and Back to home were carrying the confusion. They were the first and third most-clicked items in the overall chart.

    The sample is a real limit. Eleven people, and one of them an agent, is enough to see a size-first pattern and to hear a logic preference. It is not a map of every agent’s catalog. The ease score of 5.73, with one person at 1, says the same thing: do not treat the average as “this is fine.”

    Next, still inside what the document leaves open

    • Read the nine survey questions in full. Only their topics are in the deck: frequency, navigation preferences, pain points, ease.
    • Read the Miro notes. The canvases are in the export and the words are not legible here, so pains and delights are still unpacked.
    • Decide what Tag is for. The deck flags the mismatch and does not recommend removing it.
    • Confirm whether the parenthetical counts describe the full catalog or the filtered set.
    • Test the OR / AND rule with agents, not only with the sales roles who made up most of these 11 respondents.
    • Specify the empty state and the mobile filter. Neither is drawn.
    • Put a date range on the click charts. Only the device chart has one: January 24, 2022 – January 24, 2023.
